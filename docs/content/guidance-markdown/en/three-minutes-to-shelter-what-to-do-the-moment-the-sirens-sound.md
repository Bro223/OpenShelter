<p>In Estonia, a sounding siren carries exactly one message. It does not tell you what has happened, how long it will last or how serious it is. It says only one thing: go inside, now.</p>
<p>That is why the Rescue Board's message is deliberately blunt — you have about three minutes to shelter, and the safest place is the one you are already in.</p>
<p>**What the siren sounds like**</p>
<p>The signal is a one-minute rising and falling tone, repeated at least three times with 30-second pauses. The whole sequence lasts around four minutes. A single siren post can be heard roughly 1.5–2 kilometres away in the direction it faces, so audibility depends on buildings, weather and background noise. Indoors you may not hear it at all.</p>
<p>**Four steps, in this order**</p>
<p>1. Get inside the nearest building.</p>
<p>2. Go as low as you can — a basement, a cellar, a ground floor.</p>
<p>3. Choose a room with solid walls, away from windows and glass partitions.</p>
<p>4. Apply the two-wall rule: make sure at least two solid walls stand between you and the open air.</p>
<p>Bathrooms, WCs and stairwells are often good improvised options: they sit low, have few windows and are built from denser material. If nothing better is available, even drawing thick curtains across a window is worth doing.</p>
<p>**If you are at home**</p>
<p>Stay there. Move to the lowest floor. Do not go out looking for a public shelter: shelter spaces in Estonia are normally closed and are opened only in a war situation, and the journey itself is far more dangerous than the room you would be leaving. People who step outside to search for information, or who set off towards a distant shelter, are the ones who get hurt.</p>
<p>**If you are at work, at school or in a shop**</p>
<p>Follow the same logic: move with everyone else to the lowest floor, away from glazing. If your child is in kindergarten or school, they stay in the care of the staff there. Schools, kindergartens, care homes and hospitals are responsible for keeping the people in them safe. Do not drive over to collect them.</p>
<p>**If you are in a car**</p>
<p>On a highway, pull over next to a natural barrier — a bank, a cutting, a dense stand of trees — and shelter behind it. On a city street, park beside a building and get inside. Never leave the car in the middle of the road; emergency vehicles will need the space.</p>
<p>**If you are caught in the open**</p>
<p>Do not stay standing in the open. Lie down in a ditch, a hollow or another depression and cover your head and neck with your hands or with something soft. Fragments and objects thrown at speed by a blast cause most injuries, and staying low is the single most effective thing you can do. If the pressure wave reaches you, cover your ears with your hands and open your mouth.</p>
<p>**How long do you stay?**</p>
<p>Until official channels confirm the danger has passed. In a war situation, the guidance is to remain for a maximum of around 12 hours in a shelter space and up to 24 hours in a blast shelter. Conditions will be basic: no heating, no cooking, often no toilet, and many people in a small space. If the building takes a hit and the space is no longer safe, leave — use a secondary exit if the main one is blocked. When you come out, check your surroundings. Never touch unexploded ordnance or unfamiliar objects; report them on 112.</p>
<p>**Where the all-clear comes from**</p>
<p>EE-ALARM messages to your phone, the "Ole valmis!" and "Eesti äpp" mobile apps, ribbon text on ETV, ETV+ and the ERR news portal, the state crisis line 1247, and the pages kriis.ee and olevalmis.ee.</p>
<p>Surveys suggest that only about one person in three would actually move straight to an indoor room when the alarm sounds. Most either look for more information first or set off somewhere else. The three minutes you have are better spent going down a staircase than reading a screen.</p>
