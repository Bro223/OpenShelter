<p>Estonian civil protection uses two terms that are often mixed up. They describe two different levels of protection, and knowing the difference tells you what to expect from the space you end up in.</p>
<p>**Shelter space**</p>
<p>A shelter space is an existing building, or part of one, that has been adapted for sheltering. Its structure protects you at least from the fragments and objects thrown up by a blast. It is a practical, adapted level of protection: a strengthened basement, a reinforced stairwell, an interior room with dense walls.</p>
<p>**Blast shelter**</p>
<p>A blast shelter is a structure built specifically for sheltering. Its design protects against the explosion itself, the pressure wave, flying debris and contaminated air, including ionising radiation. It may have filtered air, sealed doors, backup power and stored water.</p>
<p>Both types come in two forms: public and non-public. Public spaces are intended for anyone who happens to be nearby; non-public spaces serve a specific building or institution.</p>
<p>**Where Estonia stands today**</p>
<p>Estonia has not built new blast shelters since regaining independence, and the state is open about that. Building a full shelter network is a project measured in decades, not years. In the meantime, the approach is to use what already exists: a large share of Estonian buildings have rooms with reinforced structures, and the task is to adapt them for sheltering.</p>
<p>Public shelter spaces are normally closed and are opened only in a war situation — so they are not a destination to travel to when an alarm sounds.</p>
<p>**What the new law changes**</p>
<p>In September 2025 the Riigikogu adopted amendments to the Emergency Act and other laws regulating the organisation of public warning and sheltering. From 1 July 2026:</p>
<p>- a public blast shelter must be built in new buildings that are visited by crowds, or whose closed net floor area is at least 10,000 m²;</p>
<p>- a non-public blast shelter must be built when a residential or other public or special-purpose building with a closed net floor area of at least 1,200 m² is erected, or an industrial or warehouse building of at least 1,500 m².</p>
<p>Rooms should also be adapted into shelter spaces in existing buildings where possible. Every blast shelter and shelter space must come with a sheltering plan. The Rescue Board coordinates sheltering activities in cooperation with local authorities.</p>
<p>**How they are marked**</p>
<p>Shelter spaces, and in future blast shelters, are marked with a blue triangle on an orange background. If you see that sign in a building, that is the room to go to.</p>
<p>**Support for apartment associations**</p>
<p>The State Shared Service Centre and the Rescue Board opened a grant round for apartment associations that want to adapt a shelter space in their building. An association can apply if the building's closed net floor area is at least 1,200 m², there are at least three apartment units, and at least 80% of the apartments are privately owned. The grant covers structural work and reinforcement, accessibility, a secondary exit, energy supply security and the purchase of equipment, as well as design work and expert advice. The maximum per applicant is €35,000.</p>
<p>**What you can do this week**</p>
<p>Find out whether your home building or workplace has a sheltering plan, or one in preparation — ask your apartment association or your employer. Then walk the route: which staircase, which door, how many seconds it takes. That single exercise turns a plan on paper into something you can actually do.</p>
