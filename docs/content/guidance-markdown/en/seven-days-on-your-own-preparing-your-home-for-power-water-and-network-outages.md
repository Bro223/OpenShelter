<p>Most crises in Estonia are not dramatic. A storm, a fallen line, a lengthy outage: for a few days, the things you never think about — water pressure, card payments, the fridge — quietly stop working. A week's worth of preparation is the difference between an inconvenience and a genuine problem.</p>
<p>The principle behind the state's advice is straightforward: be able to manage at home for at least seven days. Not because help will not come, but because during a widespread outage help will be needed in many places at once.</p>
<p>**Water**</p>
<p>Store at least three litres of drinking water per person per day, and think about cooking and basic hygiene on top of that. If your water supply depends on electricity, or you are not sure whether it does, ask your local authority or water company how supply is guaranteed during an outage.</p>
<p>If you use water from a natural source, including melted snow, bring it to a rolling boil for ten minutes or use purification products such as filters or tablets. Follow your local authority's announcements about drinking water.</p>
<p>**Sewage and sanitation**</p>
<p>Sewage may also stop during a long outage, which can lead to flooding and contamination. As an alternative, use bin bags placed inside a toilet bowl, or a dry toilet.</p>
<p>**Heating**</p>
<p>If your heating depends on electricity, work out an alternative in advance. If you have a stove, fireplace or wood-burning oven, keep the flues maintained and burn sensibly. If you have none of these, concentrate on keeping one room warm: close doors, windows and ventilation openings and seal the gaps where heat escapes. Use alternative heaters, such as gas radiators, strictly according to their instructions and fire-safety rules. If you have a generator, check that it runs and that you have enough fuel.</p>
<p>**Food**</p>
<p>Keep a store of food you can eat without cooking. A camping stove, gas burner or small grill helps, but use them outdoors only, never leave an open flame unattended, and remember that burning produces toxic gases. The best stock is made of things you eat anyway, so that it rotates naturally. Do not forget the pet.</p>
<p>**Light**</p>
<p>A torch or another alternative light source, plus a supply of batteries. Candles and gas lamps work but need to be used with care.</p>
<p>**Money and communication**</p>
<p>Keep some cash at home — roughly a week's worth of essential spending — because card terminals and ATMs may be down. Keep your power banks charged and use them sparingly.</p>
<p>In a long outage, information comes through ERR channels and Vikerraadio. A battery-powered or dynamo radio, or a car radio, will keep you informed; keep your car's fuel tank at least half full. Find out where your local resilience centre is — that is where you will get information, advice and help during a crisis.</p>
<p>**During the outage**</p>
<p>Switch off electrical appliances. If your network operator has not notified you, report the outage to your electricity network service provider. Use alternative solutions for heating, water, cooking and information. If it is cold, gather in one room, keep doors and windows shut, and open the fridge and freezer as rarely as possible. Use battery-powered devices, including your phone, sparingly and only when necessary, and switch off power-hungry apps.</p>
<p>**When to ask for help**</p>
<p>If you cannot cope safely at home, contact your local authority. That is what it is there for, and asking early is far better than asking late.</p>
<p>**A note on scale**</p>
<p>You do not need a bunker. You need a shelf, a few containers, a torch, some cash and a decision about which room you would heat and which room you would shelter in. That is genuinely most of it — and it costs one afternoon.</p>
