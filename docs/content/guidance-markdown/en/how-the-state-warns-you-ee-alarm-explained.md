<p>When people's lives, health or national security are at risk, the state warns the public directly and as widely as it can. In Estonia that system is called EE-ALARM, and it is built to work through several channels at once — because the fastest channels carry the least information, and any single channel can fail.</p>
<p>**One alert, several channels**</p>
<p>EE-ALARM combines area-based SMS messages, the "Eesti äpp" and "Ole valmis!" mobile apps, sirens, and ribbon text on ERR channels. If needed, loudspeakers, door-to-door notification and drones are used as well. During a crisis, resilience centres also share information.</p>
<p>**SMS**</p>
<p>An area-based SMS is normally sent by the agency handling the incident — the Rescue Board for a fire releasing toxic gases, the Police and Border Guard Board for a sudden attack. Messages are sent only for extensive or extreme incidents involving a direct danger to life. For Estonian SIM cards the sender is EE-ALARM; for foreign SIM cards it is the number +372 5950 0000. The message always begins with the name of the agency sending it, and it follows the language preference you have set with your mobile operator.</p>
<p>**The apps**</p>
<p>"Ole valmis!" is the Rescue Board's app, focused on preparing for crises: behavioural instructions, helpline numbers and first aid guidance. "Eesti äpp" covers state services and the main crisis instructions. The advantage of an app is that the information remains available even if the network fails.</p>
<p>**Sirens**</p>
<p>The siren network operates in 22 settlements and covers an estimated 65% of Estonia's population — including Tallinn and the surrounding densely populated areas, Tartu, Narva, Kohtla-Järve and Ahtme, Jõhvi, Pärnu, Viljandi, Rakvere, Võru, Valga, Kuressaare, Haapsalu, Kärdla, Paide, Põlva, Rapla, Sillamäe, Keila, Maardu, Paldiski, Tapa and Jõgeva.</p>
<p>Sirens are started only in extreme cases, when there is a quick and immediate danger to life. The tone carries a single message: go indoors now, away from windows, with at least two walls between you and the outside air. Sirens are designed to warn people walking in the street; indoors you may not hear them at all.</p>
<p>The signal itself is a one-minute rising and falling tone, repeated at least three times with 30-second pauses. In total it lasts about four minutes. One siren post carries roughly 1.5–2 km in the direction it faces, depending on buildings, ambient noise and weather.</p>
<p>**Ribbon text and verification**</p>
<p>Ribbon text on ETV, ETV+ and the ERR portal reaches you if you happen to be watching television or browsing the news. When an EE-ALARM alert arrives, the sender is always EE-ALARM, and the ribbon text also shows "EE-ALARM".</p>
<p>You can verify the accuracy of an alert and its instructions through kriis.ee and olevalmis.ee, by calling 1247 (from abroad +372 600 1247), and through official state websites and social media accounts.</p>
<p>**Take every alert seriously**</p>
<p>If you hear sirens, shelter first and look for information second. Siren activation is always accompanied by information through the other EE-ALARM channels.</p>
<p>**Nationwide tests**</p>
<p>EE-ALARM is tested nationwide several times a year so that the system works on the day it matters. The next test takes place on 14 October 2026 between 15:00 and 15:10; further tests are scheduled for 10 February 2027, 9 June 2027 and 13 October 2027. Around midday an SMS with advance notice of the test is sent to mobile phones. Between 15:00 and 15:10 all channels are activated, including sirens in the larger settlements.</p>
<p>Treat the tests as practice. The point of a drill is that on the day itself you are not deciding what to do — you already know.</p>
<p>A further upgrade, cell broadcast, is planned for 2027. It will deliver an alert instantly and, depending on the level of threat, make your phone alert you with sound and light even when it is set to silent.</p>
